Part A — The Simple Harmonic Oscillator (25 points)

GUIDED — Follow the Lesson

Start with a mass on a frictionless spring: the only force is the restoring force F = −kx. This is the purest test case in mechanics — and it has an exact solution you can check against.

The Physics

F = −kx    →    a = −(k/m) x = −ω₀² x
ω₀ = √(k/m)     T = 2π/ω₀
x(t) = A cos(ω₀t + φ)    (exact solution)

For initial conditions x(0) = A, v(0) = 0, the exact solution is simply x(t) = A cos(ω₀t) and v(t) = −Aω₀ sin(ω₀t).

What to do

  1. Write a function simulate_sho(method, dt, n_periods=10) that simulates the SHO using either 'euler' or 'euler-cromer'. Use k = 1.0, m = 1.0, x0 = 1.0, v0 = 0.0. Store t, x, v, and total energy E = ½kx² + ½mv² at each step.
  2. Run both methods for 10 periods with dt = 0.05.
  3. Plot x(t) for both methods on the same axes, overlaid with the exact solution A cos(ω₀t).
  4. Plot total energy vs. time for both methods on the same axes.
  5. Write 2–3 sentences: What happens to the Euler solution's amplitude over 10 periods? What happens to its energy? Does Euler-Cromer show the same problem?
💡 What you should see: The Euler amplitude grows visibly with each swing, and its energy climbs steadily upward — the oscillator is gaining energy from nowhere. Euler-Cromer holds its amplitude and keeps energy bounded (it wobbles slightly but never drifts). This is the same lesson as Lab 2, but far more dramatic because the error accumulates over every oscillation.
💡 Hint: the two integrators, side by side 
a = -(k / m) * x

if method == 'euler':
    x_new = x + v * dt      # uses OLD velocity
    v_new = v + a * dt
    x, v = x_new, v_new
elif method == 'euler-cromer':
    v = v + a * dt          # update velocity FIRST
    x = x + v * dt          # then use the NEW velocity

The only difference is whether the position update uses the old or the newly updated velocity. That one line is the difference between a stable oscillator and one that explodes.

Part B — Phase Space & Damped Oscillations (35 points)

GUIDED → INDEPENDENT

Now you'll add a new visualization tool and a new piece of physics: friction.

B.1 — Phase-space portraits

A phase-space plot shows velocity v on the vertical axis against position x on the horizontal axis, with time as an implied parameter along the curve. For the undamped SHO, this is a closed ellipse — the system returns to the same (x, v) state every period.

  1. Make a phase-space plot (v vs. x) for both Euler and Euler-Cromer from Part A, on the same axes.
  2. Write 1–2 sentences: What does the Euler curve do? What does the Euler-Cromer curve do? Connect the shape to what you saw in the energy plot.
💡 Reading phase space: A closed loop = periodic motion with conserved energy. A curve spiraling outward = energy growing (Euler). A curve spiraling inward = energy decaying. You'll use this shape-reading skill for the rest of the course.

B.2 — Add damping

Real oscillators lose energy. Add a linear damping force proportional to velocity:

F = −kx − bv    →    a = −(k/m) x − (b/m) v

Define the damping ratio ζ = b / (2√(km)). The three regimes are:

Summary of damping regimes and their effects on system behavior based on damping ratio ζ.
RegimeConditionBehavior
Underdampedζ < 1Oscillates with decaying amplitude
Critically dampedζ = 1Returns to zero fastest, no oscillation
Overdampedζ > 1Returns to zero slowly, no oscillation
  1. Extend your simulator to include the damping term. Use Euler-Cromer from here on (you've proven it's the trustworthy one).
  2. Simulate all three regimes: pick b values that give ζ ≈ 0.1 (underdamped), ζ = 1 (critical), and ζ ≈ 2 (overdamped). Keep k = m = 1.
  3. Make two plots: (a) x(t) for all three regimes on the same axes; (b) phase-space portraits for all three on the same axes.
  4. Write 2–3 sentences interpreting the phase-space plot: how does each regime spiral toward the origin differently?
💡 Try it: For the underdamped case, overlay the theoretical decay envelope Ae−γt (where γ = b/2m) on your x(t) plot. Does your simulation's amplitude follow it?
💡 Hint: choosing b for each regime

With k = m = 1, the critical value is b = 2√(km) = 2. So:

  • Underdamped ζ = 0.1 → b = 0.2
  • Critical ζ = 1 → b = 2
  • Overdamped ζ = 2 → b = 4

Part C — Driven Oscillations & Resonance (40 points)

INDEPENDENT

Now add a periodic driving force and explore resonance — the phenomenon behind everything from radio tuning to the collapse of bridges.

The Physics

F = −kx − bv + F₀ cos(ωd t)
a = −(k/m) x − (b/m) v + (F₀/m) cos(ωd t)

After transients die out, the system oscillates at the driving frequency ωd, not its natural frequency ω₀. The steady-state amplitude depends sharply on how close ωd is to ω₀. That peak is resonance.

What to do

  1. Add the driving term to your simulator. Use light damping (b = 0.2, so ζ = 0.1), F₀ = 1.0, k = m = 1 (so ω₀ = 1).
  2. Show the transient → steady state: simulate at ωd = ω₀ = 1 for many periods and plot x(t). Point out where the transient ends and steady state begins.
  3. Build a resonance curve. Sweep ωd from 0.2 to 2.0 (at least 30 values). For each, run long enough to reach steady state, then measure the steady-state amplitude (e.g., the max of |x| over the final few periods). Plot amplitude vs. ωd.
  4. Identify the peak. At what ωd does the amplitude peak? How does it compare to ω₀ = 1?
  5. Write 3–4 sentences interpreting the resonance curve: why does the amplitude peak near ω₀? What would happen to the peak height if you reduced the damping?
💡 Try it: Overlay two or three resonance curves for different damping values (e.g., b = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5). Watch the peak grow taller and sharper as damping decreases — that sharpness is the "quality factor" Q of the oscillator.
⚠️ Watch your transient. If you measure amplitude too early, you'll capture the transient, not the steady state, and your resonance curve will look noisy. Discard the first several periods before measuring. When in doubt, plot x(t) at a single ωd to confirm it has settled.
💡 Hint: measuring steady-state amplitude 
# After simulating, keep only the last ~30% of the run
n = len(x)
tail = x[int(0.7 * n):]
amplitude = np.max(np.abs(tail))

This throws away the transient and measures the settled oscillation. Make sure your total run time is long enough (many periods) for the transient to actually die out at light damping.

Bonus Challenge (up to 10 points)

⭐ The nonlinear pendulum: when the textbook formula breaks

Everything above assumed the small-angle approximation, where a pendulum is a perfect SHO with period T = 2π√(L/g), independent of amplitude. But the real pendulum obeys:

θ″ = −(g/L) sinθ

For large swings, sinθ ≠ θ, and the period grows with amplitude — something the linear formula completely misses.

  1. Simulate the full nonlinear pendulum (with sin(theta), not theta) using Euler-Cromer.
  2. Measure the period numerically for small amplitude (e.g., 5°) and confirm it matches T = 2π√(L/g).
  3. Now measure the period for large amplitudes (e.g., 30°, 90°, 150°). Plot period vs. amplitude.
  4. Write 2–3 sentences: how much does the period grow? At what amplitude does the small-angle approximation break down by more than a few percent?

This previews a deep theme of the course: linear approximations are convenient, but the interesting physics often lives in the nonlinear regime that only computation can reach.
